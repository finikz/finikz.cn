At 83, Paul McCartney has released a new album called *The Boys of Dungeon Lane*.

![Illustration](/articles/remote/5d8894442cdfd069.webp)

Dungeon Lane is not some made-up rock-and-roll place name. It is in Liverpool, near the Mersey and close to Paul’s memories of being young. When an 83-year-old turns a road from his childhood into an album title, it is easy to frame the story as “a legendary musician looking back in his later years.”

But my first reaction to the news was not nostalgia.

It was this: he is still writing about where he came from.

That is pretty remarkable.

At a certain age, “looking back” often means consuming the past. People bring out old photographs, repackage old stories and sell yesterday’s glory all over again. Rock stars are especially vulnerable to this trap. The word “Beatles” is already so big it needs no explanation; fans will welcome any scrap of the story.

But what Paul is doing this time is subtly different.

He is not just saying, “I used to be a Beatle.” He is asking who he was before the Beatles.

A few details in the album’s public story moved me.

It began with a cup of tea five years ago. Paul met producer Andrew Watt, casually picked up a guitar and landed on a chord he did not recognize himself. Watt suggested recording it. That sketch eventually became the opening track, “As You Lie There.”

Look at where the story begins: not with “a masterpiece was born,” but with an unfamiliar chord.

That is what is so appealing about a seasoned artist. They have so much certainty behind them, yet they are still willing to be led somewhere by a sound they do not recognize.

Another moment took place at Abbey Road. Paul played the new songs for 50 fans chosen in a draw. The room carries so much history: the Beatles recorded many of their classics there.

Outside, tourists may still have been taking pictures on the zebra crossing. Inside, an 83-year-old man sat in the place where he had once worked, playing songs and talking about Liverpool, John Lennon, George Harrison, his parents and his childhood after the war.

It would be easy to make this scene overly sentimental.

So I would rather think of it as a story about an “asset.”

Not a financial asset, but an asset made of memory.

We talk so fluently about content assets, brand assets, intellectual property and the long term. But what can really keep circulating over time is often not an opinion. It is where you come from.

Where did you start? Why did you become who you are? Which parts of you were planted long before you learned them deliberately?

That is the clever thing about McCartney’s new album. It is not trying to compete with young artists on “newness.” It is not pretending he is still 26. His voice has unmistakably aged; in places it is no longer as light. But the songs work because he is older. When a young person sings about the past, it can sound like a pose. When an 83-year-old sings about the past, time itself is in the room.

I like that as a business judgment too.

A truly seasoned brand does not chase youth forever. Staying relevant to young people matters, but that does not mean dressing yourself up as one of them. It means finding new ways to explain where you came from.

That is also why, around the same time, the Rolling Stones released a new album, *Foreign Tongues*, again produced by Andrew Watt. A younger producer has become the interface between these old rock stars and the sound of today. You could call it a record-industry choice, or a way for an established IP to update itself: keep its core, but find a translator the present can understand.

It makes me think of AI.

Everyone is using AI to make content now: articles, images, videos, music, ad copy and scripts. It is getting faster, more alike and nearly costless. On the surface, the barrier to creating things has fallen. In reality, that makes the genuinely scarce thing easier to see.

It is not “Can you generate something?”

It is “Do you have anything of your own to generate?”

If you have no Dungeon Lane of your own, even powerful AI can only smooth the roads other people have built. If a brand has no sense of its own origins, AI can only manufacture the average more efficiently.

McCartney certainly has material to draw on. There are the Beatles, Wings, a body of work spanning more than half a century and melodies recognized around the world. Yet this time he went further back: to Speke, to Forthlin Road, to his parents, to the days before Beatlemania.

That is not retreating.

It is bringing the roots back into view.

For people making content, building brands or pursuing independent careers today, I think there is a simple reminder here: do not only think about expressing your opinions. Take stock of where you came from.

What have you experienced in your industry that others have not? How did your taste take shape? Why do you believe certain things and dislike others? What small event once changed you?

These things may not seem useful in the moment. They cannot be monetized immediately, turned into a course tomorrow or spread as easily as a hot topic.

But over time, they become the sound that makes you different from everyone else.

Paul and Ringo sing together as genuine co-lead vocalists for the first time on “Home to Us.” That is interesting in itself. The two have made music together since 1962, yet it took until 2026 for them to have this kind of “first.” One of the most familiar pairings in the world still had something it had never done.

It gives me pause.

If you live long enough and keep making work long enough, what matters in the end may not be explosive energy. It may be whether you can keep discovering yourself.

Not by finding a completely new self, but by noticing a small road in a familiar place that you had never really looked at before.

Perhaps Dungeon Lane is such a road.

Many years ago, a boy from Liverpool walked along it, perhaps with a bird-watching book in his hand, not yet knowing who he would become. Many years later, he put that road into a song and played it for the world.

Of course, that is nostalgia.

But good nostalgia does not hide in the past.

Good nostalgia gives the past back to the present.

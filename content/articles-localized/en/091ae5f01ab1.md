You have probably seen this kind of person. Maybe you are one. I used to be one, anyway:

Their phone has a dozen AI apps. Their browser bookmarks hold links to more than three hundred tools. They have shared “100 AI tools everyone should know” on Moments. There is even a folder on their desktop called “AI Side-Hustle Materials.”

They study AI every day.

But six months later, they still do not know what they can actually use AI to do.

That is a little grim.

Because the problem may not be AI.

The problem may be that they have nothing AI can amplify.

---

Many people have a very simple misconception about AI: they think that if the tools get stronger, they will get stronger too.

It is like someone buying a full set of top-of-the-line kitchen gear—a Japanese steel knife, a cast-iron pan, a sous-vide machine, a molecular-gastronomy torch—then opening the fridge to find half a bag of frozen dumplings.

You can certainly say the tools are not the problem.

But you have no ingredients.

AI is the same.

It is a knife, a pan, a stove, an assistant chef who never gets tired of you. Ask it to chop vegetables and it does it fast. Ask it to make a sauce and it can give you ten recipes. Ask it to plan a menu and it will write one that sounds like a Michelin-starred restaurant.

But first you need to know who you are cooking for today.

You need to know what they like to eat.

You need to know why you are making this meal.

Otherwise, you just own a very advanced kitchen and stand inside it watching short videos every day.

---

I have heard so many people ask the same question:

“Is it too late for me to learn AI now?”

It is not too late.

But that is the wrong question.

A more accurate question would be:

**What do you want AI to amplify?**

Your writing ability?

Your sales ability?

Your design ability?

Your industry experience?

Your understanding of customers?

Your taste?

Your project management?

Your judgment?

If those things are empty to begin with, AI can only amplify the emptiness.

After amplification, it is still empty.

Only now you have a hundred empty bowls instead of one.

It looks busy.

---

That is why so many people get more anxious the more they use AI.

They thought AI was an elevator they could step into to go upstairs.

Then they found it is more like a megaphone.

If you have a voice, it lets more people hear you.

If you have no voice, it only amplifies the static.

That is not a pleasant thing to hear.

But the unpleasant parts are often true.

---

In the past, it was hard for an ordinary person to get anything started.

To write an article, you had to research, build the structure and revise every sentence yourself.

To make a video, you had to know how to shoot, edit, record voice-over and add subtitles.

To build a small product, you had to understand design, development, operations and customer service.

To offer consulting, you needed cases, communication skills, delivery and retrospectives.

So many people got stuck before they could even begin.

AI has made getting started easier.

That is a good thing, of course.

But it has also brought another consequence: **getting started is no longer valuable in itself.**

In the past, writing a decent WeChat article counted as a skill.

Now someone who cannot write at all can ask AI for an article that is “well structured, fluent and looks about right.”

In the past, making a poster meant people thought you had technical skills.

Now, as long as your prompt is not completely off the mark, AI can give you ten images that are visually passable.

In the past, knowing how to make a slide deck could earn you a living.

Now even the boss can ask AI to generate a first version.

That does not mean writing, design and slide decks have no value anymore.

Quite the opposite.

It means **merely making something that is more or less okay** is not worth as much as it used to be.

---

AI has lowered the entry bar.

But it has raised the bar for experts.

This is important.

Many people see only the first half, so they get excited. After they have done the work for a while, they hit the second half and start to suffer.

You find that generating a paragraph with AI is easy, but judging whether it contains insight is hard.

Generating an image with AI is easy, but judging whether it has aesthetic quality is hard.

Generating a business plan with AI is easy, but judging whether it is all theory and no practice is hard.

Getting AI to write a sales email is easy, but figuring out why the customer did not reply is hard.

AI can make you ten times faster at execution.

But if you are headed in the wrong direction, it can also make you go astray ten times faster.

It is like getting into a very fast car with a driver who is exceptionally obedient.

The problem is that you do not know the destination.

---

What ordinary people really need is not another tool to learn.

It is to gradually move from “someone who can do tasks” to “a small system that can keep producing results.”

That system needs at least four parts.

First, **you need a problem area**.

That means the problems you care about over the long term.

Not useless generalities like “I want to make money.” Everyone wants to make money. You need to say clearly whom you help and what problem you solve.

For example: help small and medium-sized business owners put AI into their business processes.

Help cross-border sellers create content and advertising materials.

Help ordinary people understand how careers are changing in the AI era.

Help people in a specific industry lower the cost of customer acquisition.

The more specific the problem, the easier it is for people to remember you.

Second, **you need judgment**.

You know what is good, what is bad, and what only looks impressive.

What AI lacks most is not output capacity.

What AI lacks most is taste and boundaries.

It can give you ten proposals, but it does not know which one suits your customer, your industry, your price range and your brand’s character best.

You have to know.

Third, **you need reusable assets**.

Articles, case studies, templates, workflows, prompts, customer FAQs, retrospectives, courses, product descriptions and project SOPs.

These things are not glamorous.

But they are the assets ordinary people can truly build up.

Many people chase trends every day: they learn this tool today, try that model tomorrow, then switch to the “best workflow” the day after. In the end, they have nothing to show for it.

Like carrying bricks.

After a year of carrying them, you look back and find the bricks are still on someone else’s construction site.

Fourth, **you need distribution and trust**.

That means: how do people know you, why do they trust you, and why would they think of you when a certain problem comes up?

One viral post will not solve this.

It takes repetition over time.

You keep putting out work around the same problem, showing your judgment, sharing case studies and proving you are not just jumping on a trend.

Over time, people start to associate you with a particular problem.

That is when AI becomes genuinely useful.

Because it starts amplifying your system instead of manufacturing an illusion for you.

---

So the one thing ordinary people learning AI should stop doing first is collecting tools.

Collecting tools is the cheapest form of self-congratulation.

It makes you feel as if you are making progress.

In reality, you are just organizing your anxiety into folders.

What you should actually do is choose one problem and work on it with AI for thirty consecutive days.

Do not get greedy.

Just one problem.

If you want to write with AI, do not spend every day studying “the ten formulas behind viral headlines.” Spend thirty days analyzing one account. Record its headlines, openings, structure, emotion, turns and comment-section responses, then use AI to help you build your own analysis template.

If you want to serve business clients, do not start by saying, “I do AI automation.” Find a real business process: organizing sales leads, a customer-service knowledge base, first-pass contract reviews, meeting notes, short-video scripts or inventory analysis. Break down how it is done now, where time is wasted, who is responsible, who approves it, and who takes the blame when something goes wrong.

That is where AI belongs.

Otherwise, you are walking around with a hammer, looking for nails everywhere.

Eventually, you start feeling like a nail yourself.

---

Many people are frustrated, not because they are not trying hard.

It is because they have put themselves in the wrong position.

The AI era is no different.

You think you are losing because you do not know how to write prompts; actually, you are losing because you have no problem area.

You think you are losing because you cannot make images; actually, you lack aesthetic judgment.

You think you are losing because you cannot write copy; actually, you do not understand your customers.

You think you are losing because you cannot build a personal brand; actually, you have not even worked out whom you want to be remembered by.

You keep learning tools, but tools only make what you already have go faster.

They cannot grow a soul for you.

---

This is not an argument against learning AI.

Quite the opposite.

Of course you should learn.

But the order in which you learn AI has to change.

Do not start with “What features does this tool have?”

Start with “What do I have that can be amplified?”

If you are a teacher, can you turn ten years of teaching experience into a reusable system for answering questions?

If you work in sales, can you turn common customer objections into a training library?

If you are a designer, can you write your aesthetic judgment into a clear set of standards?

If you are a boss, can you first map out the three company processes that waste the most labor?

If you create content, can you first decide what world you want to explain over the long term?

These things do not sound as exciting as “Learn to make money with AI in 10 minutes.”

But they are the real thing.

Because AI will keep getting cheaper.

There will be more and more models.

The tools will become easier and easier to use.

In the end, what will really be scarce is not whether you know how to click a button.

It is whether you have a capability worth amplifying.

---

The most dangerous fantasy for an ordinary person is believing that the era has handed them a cheat code, so they no longer need to practice the fundamentals.

That is not how it works.

A cheat code only helps someone who is already playing the game.

If you have not even opened the map, a cheat code will only let you fly around the starting area.

AI will not suddenly give direction to someone who has none.

AI will not suddenly give a track record to someone who has built up nothing.

AI will not suddenly make someone who does not understand customers understand them.

AI will not suddenly give someone without a body of work a portfolio.

At most, it will expose your problems faster.

That is a good thing too.

It is better to expose them sooner than to discover three years later that you have been going around in circles.

---

So before you ask “Which AI tool should I learn?” next time, ask yourself one question first:

**If AI could amplify me tenfold, what exactly would I want it to amplify?**

If the answer is empty,

do not rush to chase tools.

Go and do one concrete thing.

Write thirty articles.

Serve three clients.

Analyze ten case studies.

Build a small product.

Document one industry.

Turn what is in your head into something other people can see, use and trust.

Once you have that, AI will no longer be a toy.

It will become leverage.

Leverage does not create force.

It lets the force that already exists move something heavier.

If you have no force of your own, give yourself the longest lever you like—it is still just another stick.

And plenty of people are swinging that stick right now.

They look busy.

But really, they are just playing with a bamboo flying toy.

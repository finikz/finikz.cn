# How Communist Countries Adapted Star Wars

October 18, 2015 · Zhang Fengming

Although *Star Wars: The Force Awakens* would not arrive until the Christmas season in December, Disney, which had bought Lucasfilm, had already begun an ambitious promotional campaign. Whenever Star Wars makes news, someone digs up the comics adapted in China in the 1980s:

The first film in the series, *Star Wars: Episode IV—A New Hope*, premiered in May 1977. The comic shown above was published in December 1980. Chinese Star Wars fans have collected evidence of at least a dozen comic adaptations from the 1980s. If you are interested, search the Star Wars Chinese website for the article “A Star Wars Comic with Chinese Characteristics” and dig through the archives. But don’t say I didn’t warn you about the artwork you may find:

![Illustration](/articles/images/907681785b2ac091.jpg)

China formally joined the Universal Copyright Convention only in 1992. For more than a decade before then, it was common to turn foreign films and TV shows into books without authorization. I am sure many of you read comics as children about “Calabash Brothers vs. Saint Seiya”:

![Illustration](/articles/images/a038b4b8852f1cb7.jpg)

△ I don’t even know what to say.

A similar situation existed among China’s communist brothers in Eastern Europe. When Western film and television rights were still uncertain, these Iron Curtain countries also practiced a “take what you can” approach. In Hungary, for example, independent comic artists adapted Star Wars without permission—but what happened next was even more remarkable.

In 1982, Hungarian comic artist Attila Fazekas published his own Star Wars adaptation without authorization from Lucasfilm. It became a landmark in Hungarian comics publishing and sold 700,000 copies that year. Hungary’s population is only about ten million today!

The artistic quality of this Hungarian “bootleg” adaptation was extremely high. Many fans believe it was far better than Marvel’s first officially licensed adaptation. Here is Marvel’s official comic:

![Illustration](/articles/images/bc71fdb881177c49.jpg)

At the time, the only countries in the communist bloc with a notable comic-book scene were Hungary, Czechoslovakia, China, Serbia and Poland. Hungary’s comics were the strongest for four reasons: first, the country had a comics tradition dating back to the nineteenth century; second, its political environment was relatively open compared with those of its socialist neighbors; third, it had experienced a comics golden age in the 1970s; and fourth, science-fiction novels and film adaptations were popular. By then Attila Fazekas was already a well-known Hungarian comic artist. He had previously adapted Hollywood films and local science-fiction stories:

![Illustration](/articles/images/b3d9a654a80c3f2e.jpg)

After drawing the first Star Wars story, he went on to adapt *The Empire Strikes Back*. The film had come out in 1980, but he had not seen it; he had only a script and stills obtained by a friend. Building on the first installment, he used his imagination to create a sequel that was also remarkably good:

![Illustration](/articles/images/da4002f86295ff9e.jpg)

A “snitch” tipped off Marvel, which then held the rights to Star Wars comics. A Marvel representative showed up and stopped the planned publication of the third book, *Return of the Jedi*. But there was little the United States could do about events on the other side of the Iron Curtain, so after the first two had already been published, Marvel let the matter go. Marvel’s licensed Star Wars comics were imported into Hungary in the 1990s, but fans still regard Fazekas’s two masterpieces as classics.

As American, Japanese and Western European comics entered the market, Hungary’s own comics scene declined. Fazekas started an irregularly published comics magazine called *BOTOND*, which now sells only a few thousand copies. He says that parents once thought comics were capitalist “poisonous weeds.” But in an age when children do not read books, it is reassuring to see them holding a comic—at least it is still a book. Yes, the picture below is the Terminator:

![Illustration](/articles/images/302cf891686650df.jpg)

He also drew some pretty “ero” (erotic) work, of course not for children:

![Illustration](/articles/images/be2d7974a89e16be.jpg)

But the story was not over. Hungarian Star Wars fans had not forgotten him—and neither had Lucasfilm! In 2012, Lucasfilm officially authorized the Hungarian Star Wars Fan Club to publish 500 copies of a *Return of the Jedi* adaptation, again drawn by Attila Fazekas. Thirty years had passed in the blink of an eye, yet fans at home and abroad were still thrilled:

That is where the story ends, although one day Disney might get the urge to bring Attila Fazekas back for another go. China’s film industry is full of talk about IP and how to cash in once, twice, three, four, five or six times on spin-off products… But first, someone has to make work that fans will love for decades, right?

In 2011, I wrote a column for Hujiang French called:

**Le Beaujolais Nouveau est arrivé!**

Beaujolais Nouveau is here!

Today is November 19, 2026, once again the third Thursday in November. That line is back in season.

I first met Beaujolais at university. Stéphanie, our French teacher, brought a bottle to share in class. Drinking wine during a lesson would be unthinkable now.

Later, she took us to Carrefour to buy the new wine. Yes, the Carrefour at Wanguo Plaza.

So why does a wine meant to be drunk young have to wait for one appointed day?

## Young Wine Is Not Lesser Wine

Beaujolais is a wine region north of Lyon, next to southern Burgundy. *Nouveau* means “new.” Beaujolais Nouveau is made from grapes harvested that same year and released that year, while it is still young.

It has finished fermenting; it is not half-made wine rushed to market. Made from the Gamay grape, it is usually macerated for a shorter time, to bring out fresh fruit and a softer palate. It is not meant to spend ten years in a cellar, so there is little point in judging it by the standards of an age-worthy wine.

![People waiting beneath a stone arch at midnight to open a bottle](/articles/images/38b8952281da2396.jpg)

Beaujolais is not all Nouveau. The region’s wine trade body says this early-release wine can be made only under the Beaujolais and Beaujolais-Villages appellations. It comes in red and rosé. The region makes other wines too, including some that can age. [Inter Beaujolais](https://www.beaujolais.com/appellation/beaujolais/) explains the appellations and styles.

Because Nouveau is so famous, it can overshadow the whole region. “Best drunk young” is often simplified to “you can’t drink it after New Year,” as if turning a calendar page spoiled the wine. That goes too far.

## Friday Works Too

In 1951, French rules generally kept appellation wines off the market until December 15 of the harvest year. The Beaujolais growers’ organization asked for an exception so this early-drinking wine could be sold sooner, and got it. [The EU’s account of the wine’s history](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=oj%3AJOC_2022_473_R) traces that change.

The tradition began for a practical reason: the wine was ready to drink, and the growers wanted to sell it earlier.

It was not until 1985 that the release date was fixed as the third Thursday in November. This is no medieval festival handed down unchanged, and it is not a day when every wine is supposed to be opened.

![Friends gathered around a table, ready to share a bottle](/articles/images/5604bfbead8d58c8.jpg)

Of course, wine merchants helped promote it. But when I think back to how I first came to know this wine, I did not need to know any of that history. Stéphanie brought a bottle to class, and we shared it. Later, we went to Carrefour together to buy some.

This year’s Beaujolais Nouveau is here. When are you free?

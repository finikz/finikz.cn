### What’s an Artsy Person Doing at Maker Faire?

*6 minutes*

**What was an arts-and-literature type doing at a tech fair?**

On Father’s Day, Finikzine—an artsy account pretending to be a tech publication—went from Guangzhou to Shenzhen to visit Maker Faire. I posted photos on social media and got plenty of likes, but one friend asked: **“You’re an arts-and-literature person. What are you doing at a tech event?”**

![Image](http://mmbiz.qpic.cn/mmbiz/ibnKwnh6UsJPq42gRGyXLYCpke8cASVWmfUfa8JODKyWyteibxpW6hVzq7YTRzGlpORjuUXXpzpdCIWqXBEIk3AQ/640?wx_fmt=jpeg&tp=webp&wxfrom=5&wx_lazy=1#imgIndex=0)

All I could do was shrug. I did take part in math and computer science Olympiads as a child, but that did not get me very far. I studied literature at university—and French literature at that… But I digress. What was I doing there? First, let us see what Maker Faire actually is. Here is the event’s own description:

![Image](http://mmbiz.qpic.cn/mmbiz/ibnKwnh6UsJPq42gRGyXLYCpke8cASVWmYDsx4OmuhAOHP8lj3JZ2ia07DhIpMwUCLd8pic8cjjIxKo6lOdCvo1JQ/640?wx_fmt=jpeg&tp=webp&wxfrom=5&wx_lazy=1#imgIndex=1)

> Maker Faire, hosted by the American magazine *Make*, is the world’s largest DIY gathering. It is a stage for ideas, innovation, and creativity; a celebration of Maker culture; and a weekend carnival the whole family can enjoy.
>
> Exhibitors can showcase their technical ideas, services, and products, share innovation with the public, meet consumers, and even find investors or business partners.
>
> Visitors can enjoy a different kind of family experience, while the exhibits appeal to both makers and creative people. Artists and arts-and-literature types can keep up with trends and become cultural pioneers. Technology can broaden your horizons, and you can discover DIY projects in art, craft, science, engineering, and music.

So Maker Faire is for the whole family, and one of its important audiences is “artists and arts-and-literature types”—or, as we call them, artsy young people.

**Why should artsy people care about technological innovation?**

The French philosopher Bruno Latour once said:

> There has never been a period in art when artists did not use the most advanced technologies available, from seventeenth-century optics to nineteenth-century projection and mirrors. An artist who is indifferent to the technological changes of their own time cannot be a particularly good artist.

Think back to a world before the division between the humanities and sciences. Brilliant figures included Mozi, Zhang Heng, Shen Kuo, Leonardo da Vinci, and Benjamin Franklin. Today, disciplines are so specialized that few people can become polymaths or “Renaissance men.” But for creative people who make a living from ideas, knowing more about technological innovation and thinking more broadly through the humanities can spark new artistic ideas and reveal new ways to express them.

And here is something the great Kevin Kelly once said:

![Image](http://mmbiz.qpic.cn/mmbiz/ibnKwnh6UsJPq42gRGyXLYCpke8cASVWmTJRzPwwsIA486sics0AlhPzKj2aNCRKRSZ9IEtAZILxJN0XSV5xU2hg/640?wx_fmt=jpeg&tp=webp&wxfrom=5&wx_lazy=1#imgIndex=13)  

> Imagine that the piano had not yet been invented when Mozart lived, or that Van Gogh had lived in a time when oil paints were prohibitively expensive. Every day, children are born with tremendous talent, but the technology they need to express themselves may not yet exist. We have a duty to invent the things that have not been invented.
>
> Helping everyone find the right technology to express their talents is the most positive purpose of technology.

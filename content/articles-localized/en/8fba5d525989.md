# 100 Years Ago Today, the Titanic Sank. Less Than a Month Later, Its Story Was on Screen

*April 15, 2016 · Zhang Fengming*

![Illustration](/articles/images/1349cce1df47a249.jpeg)

On this day 104 years ago, in the early hours of April 15, 1912, the Titanic—the most expensive and largest ship in the world at the time, said to be “unsinkable”—set out on its maiden voyage and was lost forever in the cold depths of the Atlantic.

![Illustration](/articles/images/785f7353189d21bf.jpeg)

*News of the Titanic’s sinking spread around the world.*

Many people first learned about the Titanic from James Cameron’s 1997 film of the same name, and remember the young Leonardo DiCaprio and Kate Winslet along with it. There is also a famous speech:

> “We shouldn’t think that capitalism has no room for an exchange of ideas. A film called *Titanic* is about to be released—it used to be called *A Night to Remember*. It cost 250 million to make and has already earned a billion. That is venture capital, too. The film portrays in vivid detail the relationship between money and love, rich and poor, and how different people behave in a crisis.”

![Illustration](/articles/images/c7e1d3223992a2b6.jpeg)

* *A Night to Remember* (1958)

![Illustration](/articles/images/a12e0ab6568961be.jpeg)

* *Titanic* (1998)

Over the past century, countless films and television programs have told stories about the Titanic. But you may not know that the first Titanic film, *Saved from the Titanic*, premiered less than a month after the ship sank, on May 14, 1912. Its leading actress, Dorothy Gibson, was herself a survivor of the Titanic.

![Illustration](/articles/images/3617396a54787f99.jpeg)

*Poster for *Saved from the Titanic**

The sinking also cost the film industry lives. Three film professionals hired by film companies to shoot footage on board were among the dead: William H. Harbeck, who had filmed the 1906 San Francisco earthquake; D. W. Martin; and Noel Malachard, a cameraman for Pathé.

Dorothy Gibson, one of the lucky survivors, was then a 23-year-old actress with the American branch of the French film company Éclair. She was on the verge of becoming one of the highest-paid actresses.

![Illustration](/articles/images/8195c1263bef482d.jpeg)

*Dorothy Gibson*

After two months of traveling around Europe with her mother, Dorothy boarded the Titanic to return to the United States. On the night of the disaster, she happened to be playing bridge and was still awake. When the crew told passengers to evacuate, she boarded the first lifeboat launched. It could hold 68 people, but only 28 climbed aboard. Many believed the situation might improve and preferred to stay on the “safer” ship rather than drift out to sea in a lifeboat.

But after the lifeboat had rowed more than a kilometer away, the Titanic began to sink. Dorothy would never forget the screams in the distance or the people beside her trembling in the boat…

At a time when trains and ships were the main forms of transportation, maritime disasters were common subjects for films and newsreels. After the Titanic sank, images of its departure, interviews with survivors, and slides of the ship and its interior appeared everywhere—profiting from the dead. Warner Bros. even quickly bought a short film of Captain Edward John Smith on board the *Olympic*, another ocean liner he had previously commanded, and screened it in cinemas.

![Illustration](/articles/images/6c736135047adc84.jpeg)

*Newsreel about Captain Smith shown in a cinema*

Some footage was even fraudulent. It did not show the Titanic at all, but two other ocean liners of the time, the *Lusitania* and the *Olympic*. The trade magazine *The Moving Picture News* sent reporters to cinemas to expose the fake footage, reporting each case they found.

![Illustration](/articles/images/8b64907ba9283dbd.jpeg)

*The *Lusitania*, which looked much like the Titanic*

![Illustration](/articles/images/307e2b09d6184ab7.jpeg)

*An exposé in *The Moving Picture News**

Public interest in the disaster reached its peak, and within days Éclair’s executives could no longer sit still. They had already made a fairly successful newsreel of the Titanic’s departure. Now one of their own actresses was a survivor. How could they miss this opportunity?

So, only days after Dorothy came ashore from the lifeboat, she signed a contract to star in a “survival story.” Éclair producer Jules Brulatour is said to have persuaded her; at the time, the two were lovers.

![Illustration](/articles/images/2f1adaf71bbfeb94.jpeg)

*Publicity still: Dorothy points to the place where the Titanic sank on a map.*

Filming began in New York. Some shipboard scenes were shot aboard an empty vessel docked in New York Harbor. Poor Dorothy was still in shock and broke down in tears several times during the shoot.

![Illustration](/articles/images/6999952b995ede2e.jpeg)

*In this still, Dorothy wears the clothes she had on when she escaped the Titanic.*

Using a fake set to shoot what was supposed to be a “newsreel” was absurd enough, but the film also invented a story. Dorothy played a young woman returning home from studying abroad on the Titanic to marry a naval officer named Jack. She survives the disaster, but afterward her family objects to her marrying a sailor whose life is at risk. Her mother tells Jack to leave the Navy: he must choose either love or his duty as a soldier. Jack is distraught and goes to the captain for advice. The captain tells him to stay aboard and remain a proud serviceman. Jack chooses the Navy, which moves Dorothy’s father, and he finally agrees to let them marry…

![Illustration](/articles/images/0d62759c6b485b46.jpeg)

*In the final scene, Dorothy’s father agrees to the marriage.*

The film was shot in a week and ran for only about ten minutes, the standard length for an early-1910s film. *Saved from the Titanic* became the first Titanic film—and the first to cast a real survivor in the lead. It was also released in Britain and Germany, where it sold very well. Some reviews called it astonishing and realistic; others said the filmmakers were simply exploiting the dead.

![Illustration](/articles/images/94d2cc904757bd9c.jpeg)

*The film recreates the bridge game Dorothy was playing that night.*

While making *Saved from the Titanic*, Dorothy also appeared in another film, which was released in May. She was so emotionally shattered that she could no longer bear it and decided to leave acting to become a choir singer. In 1917 she married her producer and lover, but two years later the marriage was declared invalid.

Dorothy moved to Paris to start over. During the Second World War, she became a Nazi spy. In 1944, when she decided to stop, anti-fascist fighters caught her and imprisoned her in Milan, Italy. She and two cellmates escaped and made it back to Paris, but her freedom after that escape was brief. In 1946, she died of a heart attack at the Ritz Hotel in Paris, aged 56.

![Illustration](/articles/images/e28ca37c42e3647d.jpeg)

Every known print of *Saved from the Titanic* was destroyed in a fire at Éclair in 1914. Most of the production stills published in newspapers and magazines have also been lost. The images above are almost all that remain. The film has become one of the most moving and historically significant lost works of the silent era.

To close, here is a real documentary about the Titanic—the only known footage shot before the ship’s maiden voyage. Filmed by the British company Pathé, it captures the ship in its finest—and final—moments.

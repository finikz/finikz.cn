# Leonard Cohen’s Last Letter Was to His Muse

Originally published November 14, 2016 · Zhang Fengming

> First published on the WeChat account Qiyu Film (ID: cinematik).

One afternoon in March 1960, on Bank Street in London, a 25-year-old Canadian poet named Leonard Cohen left a dentist’s office after having a wisdom tooth pulled. He was heading back to his cold, damp lodgings.

Rain was about to fall again, as it so often does in London. Cohen hurried into a Greek bank nearby. A teller wore sunglasses and had a tanned complexion, so out of place in the surrounding scene. Cohen struck up a conversation and learned that the man had just returned from Greece. He asked what the weather was like there. “Spring,” the teller replied, describing the sunny beaches of Greece…

![Illustration](/articles/images/b5396fe89182fd9f.jpeg)

*Cohen’s “residence permit” on the Greek island of Hydra.*

On April 13, Cohen flew to Athens. The next day, after a five-hour ferry ride, he arrived on Hydra in the Aegean Sea. He was immediately enchanted by the island and its community of Bohemian expatriates like himself, who all spoke English to one another. An Australian writer couple, the Johnstons, warmly invited him to stay in an empty room in their home.

The island had electricity for only one hour each night. There were no telephones or cars; people rode donkeys up and down the hills. But Cohen felt the Johnstons were living exactly the life he wanted: writing novels on a warm Greek island.

![Illustration](/articles/images/08f70ff59458b3f0.jpeg)

*Cohen and Marianne travel by donkey.*

The Johnstons also introduced him to Marianne Ihlen, a Norwegian. Marianne and her husband, the Norwegian novelist Axel Jensen, had arrived on the island a few years earlier. But when their child was six months old, Jensen had an affair and left Marianne and the baby.

![Illustration](/articles/images/afde6e5071027df2.jpeg)

*Marianne, her son, Cohen and their friends.*

At midday they sat at the Katsikas brothers’ grocery shop by the harbor, waiting for the ferry from the mainland to bring letters or new artists and writers. In the evenings they sat beneath the old pine tree at the Douskos family home, talking about philosophy, politics and religion, drinking and singing. Spending nearly every hour together, Cohen and Marianne soon fell in love.

![Illustration](/articles/images/27bfadadcd934d28.jpeg)

*Cohen, Marianne and friends beneath the old pine tree at the Douskos home.*

On September 27, just days after his twenty-sixth birthday, Leonard Cohen bought a house on Hydra for $1,500. The money came from an inheritance his grandmother had left him.

A friend called it a “big deal” at the time, because buying the house meant Cohen was settling in this mysterious place and its unknown world. Cohen later said it was the wisest decision he had ever made.

![Illustration](/articles/images/27a372ada0f527c1.jpeg)

*A recent photograph of Cohen’s house.*

The three-story house had five rooms. Its whitewashed walls had turned yellow, and there was no electricity or running water. But it was enough for Cohen and Marianne to live together, and he had a private space to write. In a letter to his mother, Cohen wrote with great affection:

“The house has a large terrace looking out over magnificent mountains and many white houses shining in the sun. The rooms are spacious and cool, with French windows and thick walls. I think the house is very old, perhaps more than two hundred years. I often imagine generations of seafaring families living here. It needs some work, but if I begin now, in a few years it could become a villa. I am living up in the hills, following a way of life that has continued for centuries. All day I hear vendors passing below; their voices are like music. I get up at seven every morning and work until noon. The cool mornings are better for work, but I also like the hot wind, especially when I remember that it takes only ten minutes to walk from my door to the Aegean Sea.”

![Illustration](/articles/images/cb3df2d9301e153e.jpeg)

*The terrace of the house.*

In that house, Cohen’s life swung between extreme discipline and extreme indulgence. He might fast for days to concentrate, or drink and take drugs every day. He might spend sunny days bare-chested, squinting as he typed novels and poems, then surrender to physical pleasures at night.

![Illustration](/articles/images/b545bad04c9674ae.jpeg)

*Cohen at work.*

Cohen called Marianne his muse.

During the years he lived on the island with Marianne, Cohen wrote two novels, *The Favourite Game* and *Beautiful Losers*, and the poetry collection *Flowers for Hitler*. It was also during this period that he began writing songs and released his first two albums, *Songs of Leonard Cohen* and *Songs from a Room*.

![Illustration](/articles/images/965939f1bdf4e693.jpeg)

*The back cover of *Songs from a Room*. Marianne sits at the desk in their room.*

![Illustration](/articles/images/117cbe3b66d512b9.jpeg)

*The house today.*

For example, “Bird on the Wire” came about after telephone poles were put up on the island. One day Marianne saw a bird on the wire outside the window that looked like a musical note. She suggested that Cohen write a song about a bird on a wire, and one of his early classics was born. Marianne always felt it was her song, too.

As Cohen’s work became more famous, he traveled more often. Sometimes they could not afford to travel together, so Marianne had to stay alone on the island. Waves of loneliness would come over her. She later said that at the time she wanted to lock Cohen in the house and swallow the key.

![Illustration](/articles/images/78ec9fe566ea16ca.jpeg)

*Marianne*

The distance between them grew, and they eventually separated. Marianne returned to Oslo in the 1970s and married again.

“I feel lucky to have met Leonard at that moment in my life,” Marianne said. Before they had truly separated, she had already heard Cohen sing “So Long, Marianne.” “He taught me so much. I hope I inspired him to write a line or two.”

After leaving Greece, Cohen never wrote another novel.

*Cohen returned to Hydra in 1988.*

That July, Marianne was in Oslo, Norway, in the final stage of cancer. Her friend Morseth contacted Cohen in California and told him Marianne might not have long to live. Two hours later, Cohen wrote this beautiful farewell letter to his muse:

“Well, Marianne, the time has come. We are really old now and our bodies are falling apart. I think I will follow you very soon. I am right behind you; if you reach out, I think you can take my hand. You know I have always loved your beauty and your wisdom. I don’t need to say any more, because you know that. Now I just want to wish you a safe journey. Goodbye, old friend. Endless love, and see you along the way.”

Morseth read the letter aloud to Marianne, who was still fully conscious. When he reached “if you reach out,” she stretched out both hands.

Two days later, she lost consciousness and died.

Morseth wrote back to Cohen:

“As she was dying, I hummed ‘Bird on the Wire’ to her, because it was the song she knew best. Then I kissed her forehead, left the room and said, ‘So long, Marianne.’”

Cohen followed Marianne, just as he had predicted. He died on November 7.

I always thought I was a gypsy boy / until I let you take me home

You know I love to live with you / but you make me forget so much

I forgot to pray for the angels / and then the angels forgot to pray for us…

When we met we were almost young / deep in the green lilac park

You held on to me as if I were a crucifix / we knelt through the long, dark hours…

Now I need your hidden love so much / I am as cold as a new razor blade…

So long, Marianne / it’s time that we began

to laugh and cry, cry and laugh about it all again…

—“So Long, Marianne”

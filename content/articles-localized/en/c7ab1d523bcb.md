# Did You Know Africa Has Its Own Hollywood?

Originally published January 4, 2016 · Zhang Fengming

![Illustration](/articles/images/577c3e5ca626fd13.jpg)

***

Everyone knows Bollywood, India’s turbocharged version of Hollywood. It had already raced past Hollywood in output value to become the world’s largest film industry. But the runner-up may surprise you: it is Africa’s Hollywood—Nollywood, in Nigeria (also called Nolly or Naija cinema).

![Illustration](/articles/images/b0c5379eba34334b.jpg)

By output volume, Nollywood had passed the other two “woods” years ago. China made just over 600 films in 2014, while Nollywood shot 1,844 films in 2013 alone. Imagine a Nigerian living in Guangzhou watching one film at breakfast, lunch, afternoon tea, dinner and a late-night snack: even after 365 days, they would never run out of domestic films to eat with…

Still, even our well-informed readers with a taste for every kind of offbeat fare have probably never heard of Nollywood, let alone seen one of its films—even though it has been around for more than twenty years…

***

1992 was a springtime year… A Nigerian named Kenneth Nnebue brought a batch of blank videotapes home from Taiwan. But selling blank tapes alone did not bring in much money. He thought he might sell more if he put something on them, so he co-wrote a script and spent $12,000 and one month making a film called *Living in Bondage*.

![Illustration](/articles/images/34bb615df934aeb5.jpg)

*The videotape cover for *Living in Bondage**

The plot was basically a Nigerian pulp story: a man becomes obsessed with witchcraft and sacrifices his wife to gain wealth. The film was extremely amateurish, with no fancy effects at all. Yet its tapes flew off the shelves—nearly a million copies sold! Historians call this the birth year of Nollywood.

The business was so profitable that Nigerians rushed to copy it. Whether they had experience or not, they picked up a camera and started filming. The average film cost only **$15,000** and could be shot in as little as **five to seven days**, with returns of at least ten times the cost. Everyone wanted a piece of the action. The stories were all pulp melodrama: poor people striking it rich, witches casting spells, family feuds and other outrageous plots. But good almost always triumphed over evil—if it had not happened yet, it was because a sequel was coming…

Nollywood had just one business model: make films on videotape and sell them locally. Nigeria did have cinemas, but there were very few, and they showed Hollywood blockbusters at prices ordinary people could not afford. Civil war and security problems had nearly eliminated cheap outdoor screenings. With an average daily income of less than a dollar and a life expectancy of about fifty, watching videotapes at home was the safest option.

***

Nollywood grew wildly for more than a decade without foreign investment or government support, amid civil war and economic hardship. Videotapes gradually gave way to VCDs. By 2005, ten years ago, Nollywood was making about 2,000 films a year and was worth $250 million. Africa’s film industry had become a system of its own and even had its own “Oscars,” the Africa Movie Academy Awards.

![Illustration](/articles/images/2b9057ba04dfea05.jpg)

*The Africa Movie Academy Awards have been presented since 2005.*

At that point, Franco Sacchi, an Italian producer living in Boston, heard about the amazing Nollywood. He quickly brought two friends to its heartland, Lagos, Nigeria’s largest city, and interviewed many filmmakers and actors. Compared with Hollywood’s huge productions, the industry was more like a small handmade workshop, but everyone smiled like the African sun and felt optimistic about the future.

![Illustration](/articles/images/bc79fd797cfca327.jpg)

This veteran director made a prophecy: “When we were young, we grew up watching American westerns, Indian musicals and Chinese kung-fu films. Now we have Nollywood, and I’m proud! In ten more years, we’ll overtake Hollywood—hahahaha!”

![Illustration](/articles/images/429ea7c693bf6151.jpg)

A confident woman director put it this way: “We make films for ordinary people, not for the elite and those who live in glass houses. If they want to watch *RoboCop* or something else, let them.”

![Illustration](/articles/images/f39494a08e087a70.jpg)

Franco also followed a film crew making an “action movie” called *Checkpoint*, about police corruption. The crew had fifty people and expected to shoot for nine days—a “big production” by local standards. They had no studio, so they filmed interiors in houses and hotels.

![Illustration](/articles/images/451e5ff738d9bd6b.jpg)

For outdoor scenes, the crew and equipment could just about fit into one van. If it rained, they had to wait for clear weather. If a village church or mosque broadcast prayers over loudspeakers, they had to stop because they could not record clean sound. The shoot ultimately took eleven days, a record for the crew.

![Illustration](/articles/images/062f5665a019d905.jpg)

This director looks like a gang boss, and he has his own view of Nollywood: “Here, if you make a film and it sells well, you immediately start shooting the next one. If you don’t, you don’t eat. We want ‘edutainment.’ I believe in the power of film. Whatever a movie is about, it should contain an idea that makes people think. The issue now isn’t whether the quality is good or bad; quality will improve over time. But first, remember the society we live in. Many people in Africa live on just one dollar a day, and they are the real audience for these films.”

Franco edited his Nollywood adventure into a documentary, *This Is Nollywood*, released in 2007. It was the first time many Western viewers learned about Nollywood. In 2008, Franco was invited to speak at TED and made a highlights version of *This Is Nollywood* for the audience.

One line from Franco’s talk sums up Nollywood—and the essence of cinema—very well:

“When you get stuck in traffic in Lagos, you might buy a movie, or a banana, or water. Yes, I have to say, this really proves that **storytelling is a commodity, a necessity; without stories, there is no life.**”

A few years later, DVDs replaced VCDs. Nollywood films became more professional, covered a wider range of subjects and reached more people. Even Japan, which has always prized professionalism, began looking toward Nigeria. In 2013, Japanese broadcaster WOWOW followed the production of *Black Silhouette*, a new film by young Nigerian director Charles Uwagbai.

![Illustration](/articles/images/2ef0f7445448abc3.jpg)

![Illustration](/articles/images/53d913b62e088eec.jpg)

The crew still packed people and equipment into a beat-up van for location shoots, but the subject, equipment and production process were already professional in every way.

The film dealt with social issues—including childhood sexual abuse and prostitution—that Nollywood had never addressed before. A professional screenwriter wrote the script, and the producer could bring in another writer to polish it if needed.

The young director spent his small budget on professional actors and renting a hotel as a set. But the film’s socially important, barrier-breaking subject persuaded some idealistic filmmakers to work for free. He also tried to cut the catering budget by serving bread instead of rice. The camera operators would not compromise on meals, though.

Despite all the difficulties, filming finished on schedule in ten days. The whole process was tight on resources but full of joy. In particular, the documentary’s moving narration by Ryunosuke Kamiki (!!) makes you admire these young Nollywood filmmakers even more:

> “Cities and societies are changing at astonishing speed. Films that audiences eagerly await cannot fall behind. Nollywood cinema draws close to people’s anxieties and loneliness, and gives them dreams.”

Piracy is now the most serious obstacle to Nollywood’s growth. Although the industry is valued at $3.3 billion, less than 1% comes from legitimate box-office revenue and royalties; the rest comes from illegally distributed DVDs, which sell for about $2 each. Nollywood is fighting piracy while also trying to stem losses through video-on-demand and actively expand beyond Africa.

![Illustration](/articles/images/7b404a349cc0c2d9.jpg)

*On the set of *October 1**

Since last year, some new Nollywood films have entered the U.S. market. Kunle Afolayan’s thriller *October 1*, for example, was released worldwide exclusively by Netflix. Netflix had already released ten Nollywood films. It also paid $12 million for the rights to adapt the Nigerian novel *Beasts of No Nation*; the film premiered at Venice this year and won widespread praise. Chinese filmmakers have even begun traveling to Nollywood to take part in producing and shooting films. Before long, we may see Nollywood films on Chinese video platforms.

# 120 Years of Cinema: How the French Celebrated

Originally published December 29, 2015 · Zhang Fengming

![Illustration](/articles/images/5cbeba8844d5f377.jpg)

A few days ago, the Institut Lumière in Lyon gathered 3,000 people to walk out of the Institut Lumière—the former Lumière factory—recreating the scene of the world’s first film, *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*.

![Illustration](/articles/images/b30d1b46bdd752a5.jpg)

*The Lumière factory in 1895 vs. the Institut Lumière in 2015.*

On December 28, 1895, the Lumière brothers held the first public, ticketed screening of their silent short films at the Salon Indien of the Grand Café, 14 Boulevard des Capucines in Paris. That day marks the birth of cinema.

![Illustration](/articles/images/88278481c3ad5473.jpg)

*A poster for a Lumière film.*

France celebrated the 120th anniversary of cinema all year, led by the Institut Lumière. Events included a retrospective called *Lumière! The Adventure Begins* at the Grand Palais in Paris and three city walks in Paris and Lyon retracing the Lumière brothers’ footsteps. The most significant event, though, was the restoration of their films and the release of a Blu-ray set.

The restoration cost €100,000, funded by the Total Foundation and the National Heritage Foundation. The Institut Lumière worked with several film archives and leading restoration experts to restore 150 Lumière shorts in 4K. In September, it released a two-disc Blu-ray collector’s edition, *Lumière!*. It was the first Blu-ray release of the Lumières’ work and came with a 44-page booklet.

![Illustration](/articles/images/20f378236b341012.jpg)

*The *Lumière!* Blu-ray set.*

Lumière cameramen traveled beyond Europe and America to Asia, Africa and Latin America, making more than 1,500 short films. A total of 1,422 survive. As the first volume, *Lumière!* contains 114 shorts made between 1895 and 1905. Each runs about 50 seconds, for a total of 95 minutes.

![Illustration](/articles/images/3587eea4ff97d6cb.jpg)

*Blu-ray navigation menu.*

The original negatives came from the Institut Lumière, the Cinémathèque française and the French National Film Archives. They were first scanned in 4K by the respected Immagine Ritrovata film restoration laboratory in Bologna, Italy, then restored by the laboratory of Eclair Group, a venerable French film company with a century of history.

The restored version premiered at the Cannes Film Festival in May. Louis Lumière chaired the first Cannes festival, so the screening also paid tribute to the Lumière brothers. Thierry Frémaux, artistic director of both the Institut Lumière and Cannes, said: “The restoration is of outstanding quality. It fully reveals the original splendor of the Lumière films, their shots and their depth of field.”

The feature has three audio tracks. The first is silent, as the films originally were. The second adds music by Saint-Saëns. The third includes both Saint-Saëns’s score and commentary by Frémaux.

Among the special features, viewers can see a living Auguste Lumière and watch a 1968 conversation in which Éric Rohmer invited the great director Jean Renoir and film obsessive Henri Langlois—the founder of the Cinémathèque française and a guardian of the Lumière negatives—to talk about Lumière’s films.

The most fun feature brings together six contemporary directors—Pedro Almodóvar, Michael Cimino, Xavier Dolan, Jerry Schatzberg, Paolo Sorrentino and Quentin Tarantino. Each remade *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*, and all six films play simultaneously in a single frame. Dolan even insisted that every actor leaving the factory gate carry a mobile phone.
